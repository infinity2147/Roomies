# Skip graph RL, scale data and LoRA

Our best-supported plan is to spend almost nothing on learned code-graph traversal, to spend the GPU hours on a better-tuned LoRA SFT run over broader, cleaner data, and to use the localization work only as a small, publishable CPU-side artifact. Published learned localizers (RL-trained 7B-14B agents, retrieve-and-rerank) match Claude-3.5/3.7-class function localization, but they move end-to-end resolve rate by only **+2 to +6 points**, and A2Agent's oracle-localization gap is about 8 points. Our own diagnosis (149 of 171 failed runs already read the gold file) puts us at the low end of that range, so a LoRA-GRPO localization policy on the 31B model is not worth its cost. For fine-tuning, the evidence supports our rank-128, alpha-256 setup but argues for more epochs, a smaller effective batch, loss masking of malformed turns, and a length curriculum. For data, the cleanest additions are `nvidia/SWE-Hero-openhands-trajectories` and `SWE-Lego` real data, deduplicated against the nebius set and capped at about 3 trajectories per task. Several load-bearing facts are unverified: the unread dataset cards, a throughput figure that does not reconcile, and no study of LoRA on a QAT int4 base. Those are listed where they matter. The paper-worthy angle is a budget-capped localization benchmark under degraded graphs, plus a transfer-robustness data resource.

## Learned localization gives small gains, so build a cheap non-learned prior first

The literature is consistent. RL-trained small agents reach near-frontier function localization in few calls. RepoNavigator trains GRPO from Qwen2.5-7B/14B/32B with a single jump-to-definition tool, group size 8, at most 12 tool calls, and reward 0.5×DICE plus 0.5×tool-call success ([RepoNavigator](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20957)). It uses about 2.7k tokens per task against 48.9k for LocAgent. CodeScout trains Qwen3-14B with GSPO on 9,600 SWE-smith instances using only bash tools (rg, find, grep, ls) and no graph, with a multi-level F1 reward plus a turn-completion term ([CodeScout-14B](https://huggingface.co/OpenHands/CodeScout-14B)). ToolTrain combines SFT on 5k teacher trajectories with RL on nDCG@k, and its 7B file Recall@5 rises from 57.7 (base) to 83.1 (SFT+RL) ([ToolTrain](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03012v2)). A2Agent adds per-turn advantages and a "discovery-commit" reward (explored gold nodes that actually reach the answer), but we only have a secondary summary of it ([A2Agent summary](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.29831.md)). The non-agentic alternative is stronger per call: SweRank retrieves and reranks with a trained listwise reranker, reaching **88.7 function Acc@10 on SWE-bench Lite for about $0.01 per task**, against $0.66 for a Claude-3.5 LocAgent run ([SweRank](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07849)).

The end-to-end deltas explain why we should not invest heavily. LocAgent's fine-tuned 32B lifts Pass@10 repair from 33.6 to 36.1 ([LocAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09089)). SHERLOC reports +5.95 points resolve on average when locations and diagnoses are handed to repair agents ([SHERLOC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24820)). RepoNavigator's 14B improves Verified resolve from 10.1 (Agentless) to 14.7–15.0 ([RepoNavigator](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20957)). The reference RL recipes also assume cluster-scale budgets (4k–10k training instances, 8–16 A100-80G class), and our frozen int4 31B base with a shared main-agent LoRA would have to share a single adapter between localization and repair. Two design lessons from the papers do transfer to our setup. First, fewer tools help small models: RepoNavigator's 7B scores 24.3 function IoU with one tool and 13.7 with four. Second, generalization drops sharply on unseen repos, with function F1 falling roughly by half on SWE-bench Pro (27–34 to 14–21). That drop is the closest published evidence for the hidden-repo risk, though no paper ablates a missing or stale graph, and none tests int4-quantized models.

Because organizer graphs may be empty on hidden repos, the first deliverable should be a self-built graph tool that does not depend on them. This is our inference, not a published recipe. A single tool call would walk `/workspace` with Python `ast` (definitions, name-based calls, imports, inheritance), run BM25 over function text, docstrings and paths, seed from identifiers and tracebacks in the issue, expand 1–2 hops with a decay-weighted score, and return a ranked top-10 with three-line snippets. It matches the lexical-anchor active-set idea in LARGER, which reports +13.9 file Acc@5 on LocBench over its strongest tuned baseline ([LARGER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16352)), and the hop expansion in CoSIL and RepoGraph. Plain BM25 reaches only 31.8 function Acc@5 on Lite against 64–73 for agents, so there is headroom to close ([LocAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09089)). Cost is 1–2 days of CPU work. Evaluate it first as a Hit@k-versus-calls measurement on our 260 tasks, especially the 22 failures that did not read the gold file; only if it helps there should it become a tool. The optional second step is a LightGBM/LambdaRank frontier reranker over features such as BM25, embedding similarity, hop distance, edge type and symbol overlap, with labels mined cheaply from patch diffs. It trains on CPU in minutes, costs 2–4 days, and we expect **+0 to +3 resolve points**, an extrapolation from the cited deltas. A LoRA-GRPO localization policy on the 31B is the item to skip: rollout cost on 4×L4 and trainer throughput make it a multi-day GPU commitment bounded by the same small gain.

## LoRA settings: keep rank 128, raise epochs, shrink the batch, mask bad turns

The most relevant evidence is indirect, because nearly every open SWE-agent SFT recipe uses full fine-tuning (SWE-Lego, SERA, Nebius RFT, SWE-Gym OpenHands). The one LoRA data point is SWE-Gym's Moatless run: rank 64, LR 5e-4, batch 8, 5 epochs, and an OpenHands verifier trained with LoRA that matched or beat its full-parameter counterpart (29.8@8 vs 27.2@8) ([SWE-Gym](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21139)). For general code, Biderman et al. find that LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning at low ranks, that ranks 16–64 are insufficient for code, that attention-only targeting is worse than MLP or all-linear, and that alpha=2r is crucial at high rank. They recommend all modules, rank 256 if memory allows, and the highest stable LR from a sweep; at r=256 an LR of 2e-4 spiked while 1e-4 was stable ([Biderman et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)). Thinking Machines puts the optimal LoRA LR at about 10× full-FT, nearly independent of rank under 1/r scaling, warns that LoRA tolerates large batches poorly (effective batch under 32), and finds MLP targeting necessary ([TRL guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/lora_without_regret)). That work is lab-internal with no independent replication. Our pilot (r=128, alpha=256, all modules, LR 1e-4) sits inside that envelope. Full-FT LRs for 32B SFT run 1e-5 to 5e-5, so the 10× rule gives 1e-4 to 5e-4. We recommend keeping 1e-4 and testing 2e-4 only if a batch of 8–16 windows shows no spikes.

The pilot's real weak spots are epochs and batch size. One epoch over about 1,000 windows is only 30–125 optimizer steps at a batch of 8–32, which is thin for a method whose appeal is also that it "forgets less" and so may under-learn. Published recipes use 3–5 epochs on 5–18k trajectories (SERA 3, SWE-Lego 4, SWE-Gym up to 5), and Nebius used one epoch at a very low LR of 5e-6. We recommend 2–3 epochs with a held-out scaffold-fidelity eval to stop early, because overfitting at LoRA scale on 1k windows is unmeasured. Variants are not worth the time: a large hyperparameter-search study finds DoRA, PiSSA, LoRA+ and others all peak within 1–2% of vanilla LoRA once LR is tuned per method ([Learning Rate Matters](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.04998v1)), and batch size explains most conflicting variant results ([Beware of the Batch Size](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09492)). PiSSA and LoRA-GA need SVD or gradient initialisation of a quantized base, and DoRA's vLLM support is unchecked, so skip all three. Keeping the adapter separate from the int4 base, as we do, avoids the accuracy loss documented for merging QLoRA adapters into 4-bit weights ([Kaitchup](https://kaitchup.substack.com/p/qlora-merging-pitfalls-dont-lose)). The remaining risk is train-serve numerics, so compare logits between the trainer's dequantization and vLLM's int4 kernel. We found no study of LoRA over QAT int4 specifically, and none on K=V-tied attention, so copying k_proj LoRA to v_proj must be verified numerically.

Loss design is where the best-measured gains sit. SWE-Lego masks loss on agent turns that triggered invalid tool-call errors (regex-detected), but not on reproduction or test failures. Its ablation shows resolve of 42.2/52.6 (8B/32B) with masking plus curriculum, 41.8/51.8 masking only, 40.2/50.4 curriculum only, and 39.4/48.8 with neither, so each is worth roughly 1–2 points ([SWE-Lego](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01426)). Its curriculum bins trajectories by turn count (0–50, 50–70, 70–100) in three cumulative stages, and trajectory length correlated at r=−0.95 with resolve rate. Step Rejection FT keeps all steps as context and zeroes loss on harmful steps in unresolved trajectories (found by an LLM critic), adding +1.3 points over plain rejection FT at about $660 per 5,000 trajectories for the critic ([SRFT](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10674)); we do not need the critic, since regex masking captures most of the benefit cheaply. For our 24k-token windows cut from longer trajectories, we recommend zero loss weight on the carried-over earlier turns and loss only on the later turns, so overlapping windows do not train any turn twice. Per-trajectory versus per-token normalization and thinking-token handling have no cited evidence; match the deployed chat template exactly and include thinking tokens in loss only if the served model emits them. Our own 100-call, 5-minute serial budget favors shorter trajectories, so SERA's finding that ordered truncation at about 0.95 coverage beats random truncation ([SERA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20789v2)) supports preferring trajectories of 32k tokens or fewer.

On RFT versus RL, the evidence favors iterated rejection sampling for now. Published SWE RL runs are full-parameter on clusters: DeepSWE used 64 H100s for 6 days on 4.5k R2E-Gym problems ([Together](https://together.ai/blog/deepswe)), and Nebius DAPO used 128 H200s ([Nebius](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03501v2)). Rank-1 LoRA matches full fine-tuning in RL on non-SWE tasks, but we found no LoRA-GRPO SWE result at 30B. A back-of-envelope check shows why it is out of reach: a 64-trajectory batch at 20k tokens is about 1.3M tokens, which at our measured 60–110 tok/s is 3.5–6 A100-hours per step per GPU. SWE-Gym's self-improvement round saturated after one iteration (Moatless 19.0 to 19.7), so a single RFT pass on our own verified rollouts, used as a minority share of the mix, is the defensible next step, while Nebius's RFT-then-RL jump from 20% to 39% marks the ceiling a larger setup could reach.

Throughput deserves a correction before planning. Our notes cite 16M tokens in about 33 hours, which implies about 135 tok/s average, above the measured 60–110 tok/s range; at 60–110 tok/s on one A100, 16M tokens takes **40–74 hours**, so either multiple GPUs were used or the figures need rechecking. The most reliable speedup is data parallelism: the frozen int4 base takes 16–20 GB per GPU and LoRA gradients are tiny, so scaling across 4 A100s should be near-linear (our inference). Fused linear cross-entropy such as Liger avoids materializing full logits, which is a large cost with Gemma's roughly 262k vocabulary (an unverified estimate: about 25 GB of fp32 logits at 24k tokens), but Liger's published gains (+20% throughput, −60% memory) were measured on full-FT multi-GPU setups, not single-GPU LoRA ([Liger Kernel](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.10989)). Our windows are already near full length, so packing buys little; length bucketing avoids padding waste. No published tokens-per-second figure for 30B+ LoRA at 24k on A100 exists to benchmark against.

## Data: add SWE-Hero and SWE-Lego first, keep a 3-per-task cap, hold out repo diversity

Data volume is the best-evidenced driver of gains. SWE-Gym's 491 trajectories lifted Qwen2.5-Coder-32B from 7.0 to 20.6 on Verified ([SWE-Gym](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21139)). SWE-smith reached 40.2 with 5,016 trajectories and scales roughly with log(repos) while capping each task at 3 trajectories because repeated easy solves hurt ([SWE-smith](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21798v2)). SWE-Lego reached 52.6 from about 18k validated trajectories ([SWE-Lego](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01426)), and Kwai-Klear reports near-linear gains in log(#trajectories) from 1k to 66k ([mirror card](https://gitcode.com/hf_mirrors/Kwai-Klear/SWE-smith-mini_swe_agent_plus-trajectories-66k)). Our roughly 1,000 windows sit at the low end, and since the hidden set spans about 120 unseen tasks across private repos, repo diversity probably matters more than raw volume (our inference; no repo-scaling ablation was found). The table ranks candidates for our setup; all OpenHands-format sets need the same tool mapping already done for nebius (execute_bash / str_replace_editor to run_command / read_file / edit_file / write_file / submit_patch).

Three caveats limit this ranking. Several cards (SWE-smith-trajectories, Skywork-SWE, Kwai-Klear, SWE-Lego) were not fetched directly, so their licenses and exact ids are secondary or unconfirmed. SWE-Hero, Nemotron-SWE-v1 and nebius share teachers and issue pools (SWE-Gym, R2E, SWE-rebench), so stacking them without deduplicating on instance_id or repo plus base_commit would silently multiply easy tasks. And none of these sets states test verification, so filtering on a resolved field, where present, or re-verifying is required. For license cleanliness, SWE-Hero's repo-license filter makes it the cleanest, and SERA's "research and educational use" wording is ambiguous for a competition that may require open release; CC BY 4.0 is a data license rather than an OSI software license, so confirm against the competition rules. Qwen3-Coder and GLM-4.6 output-use terms were not checked.

On mixing, no published study fixes an own-versus-external ratio. The closest evidence is SERA, where 16k T2 plus 9.2k T1 (49.00%) nearly matches 25k T2 (49.53%) and clearly beats 16k T2 alone (47.07%) ([SERA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20789)), and SWE-TRACE, where shortest-path traces beat raw successful rollouts (4B 33.6 to 37.8) ([SWE-TRACE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.14820)). A defensible starting point, flagged as our hypothesis, is to use external data as the bulk for tool-use breadth and format, keep verified own-scaffold rollouts at 20–40% as the format anchor, cap each task at 3 trajectories, prefer shorter trajectories, and run a parse-validator on every assistant turn so format-breaking examples are dropped or masked. Format is a leading failure mode in published work: Nebius's base 72B scored about 11%, mostly from command-formatting errors ([Nebius](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.03501)), so tool-call parse rate on rollouts, not loss, should gate adapter acceptance. For the hidden google-style repos, we found no dataset documenting absl, gin or jax coverage; grep the SWE-rebench `repo` column for `google/`, `abseil` and `google-deepmind/`, and otherwise generate tasks with SWE-smith.

## Ranked plan and what is paper-worthy

The ranking below weighs expected gain against cost on our hardware. Gains are rough and, except where cited, our extrapolations.

| Rank | Action | Cost | Expected gain | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Train with loss masking on malformed-parse turns, loss only on non-carried turns, 2–3 epochs, effective batch 8–32; evaluate by parse rate and a held-out scaffold eval | Hours of config, one rerun (40–74 h per GPU at 16M tokens) | +1 to +3 | SWE-Lego ablation; Biderman; TML |
| 2 | Add deduplicated SWE-Hero (then SWE-Lego real) with a 3-per-task cap and own rollouts at 20–40% | 1–2 days of conversion plus longer training | +2 to +5 (data scaling, undampened by our stronger base) | SWE-Gym, SWE-smith, SERA |
| 3 | Length curriculum (3 cumulative bins by turns) | Near zero | +0.5 to +2 | SWE-Lego |
| 4 | Data-parallel across 4 A100s, fused cross-entropy, length bucketing | 1 day | 2–4× faster iteration | Inference; Liger |
| 5 | AST+BM25+hop-expansion localization tool, evaluated on the 22 non-gold-read failures first | 1–2 days CPU | 0 to +2; robust to empty graphs | LARGER, CoSIL; localization-gap papers |
| 6 | One iteration of RFT on own verified rollouts, fed back as the minority share | Rollout GPU time | Small, saturates after one round | SWE-Gym |
| 7 | LightGBM/LambdaRank frontier reranker trained from patch-diff labels | 2–4 days CPU | 0 to +3 | LocAgent, SHERLOC deltas |
| 8 | LoRA-GRPO localization or main-agent RL on the 31B | Days of GPU | Unproven | Skip |

The paper track is the place where the localization work earns its keep, not the leaderboard. The gap we can claim, subject to a proper literature check (our search was only about 19 calls and did not examine GFlowNet or MCTS localization), is that no work reports budget-capped Hit@k-versus-tool-calls curves across BM25, retrieve-rerank, graph-expansion and agent methods, including degraded-graph conditions (organizer graph versus rebuilt AST graph versus none) and an int4 quantized policy. Closest prior art is A2Agent (agentic RL with graph traversal), LARGER (heuristic frontier), GREPO (GNNs without an agent loop) and SweRank (rerank without traversal). A learned frontier reranker trained from patch labels and transferred across repos, together with a measured "localization is not the bottleneck" analysis (failure-mode breakdown at our scale against an oracle-localization gap), is a defensible Best Paper angle; the failure-mode analysis alone is a cheap, honest contribution. For Best New Resource, the strongest candidates are a released deduplicated, scaffold-validated mixture of the permissively licensed trajectories converted to a single tool schema with parse-validity flags and a repo-license manifest, and a released, small-hardware reproducible LoRA recipe (rank, LR, masking, curriculum, QAT-serving numerics check) for a 31B int4 agent. The resource claim depends on resolving the license questions above.

## Conclusion

The evidence reorders our priorities: localization research is the best paper and the weakest score lever, while loss masking, more epochs at a smaller batch, and broader deduplicated data are the cheapest score levers and carry no novelty. Our setup also sits in an under-studied regime, a frozen int4 QAT base with a separately served LoRA, where nothing published tests train-serve numerics, K=V-tied attention LoRA, or localization robustness to a missing graph; each of those is both a risk to check before spending training hours and a potential contribution.

The uncertainties that could change this plan are concrete and cheap to resolve: whether the 22 non-gold-read failures benefit from the graph tool, whether the SWE-Lego and Skywork licenses permit open release, whether the 135 tok/s figure or the 60–110 tok/s range is right, and whether 2–3 epochs overfit at 1k windows. Settle those four before committing the multi-day GPU run.
